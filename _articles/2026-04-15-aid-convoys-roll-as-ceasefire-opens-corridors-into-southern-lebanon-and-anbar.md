The first United Nations-coordinated aid convoys rolled into southern Lebanon and western Iraq within hours of the Iran ceasefire taking effect at midnight Greenwich Mean Time on Wednesday, as relief agencies raced to stabilize more than four million displaced people and to begin assessing return corridors that have been sealed or unsafe for the better part of six weeks.

A column of 22 trucks carrying generators, medical supplies and high-energy food rations crossed the Litani River bridge at Khardali at 9:14 a.m. local time, the first humanitarian convoy to reach the south of the country since Israeli air operations expanded north of the river in late March. A separate 14-truck convoy moving south from Erbil along the Mosul-Ramadi axis reached the outskirts of Hit, in Iraq's Anbar province, by early afternoon, accompanied by an Iraqi army engineering detachment surveying for unexploded ordnance. Smaller deliveries reached Saada governorate in northwestern Yemen and a transit reception site outside the Iranian city of Bandar Abbas, where roughly 38,000 industrial workers and their families have been sheltering since the heaviest U.S. and Israeli strikes on the southern coast.

"The first day of a ceasefire is not the end of an emergency. It is the moment when the emergency becomes legible," U.N. Emergency Relief Coordinator Joyce Msuya told a hastily convened briefing in Geneva. "We can finally see the shape of what we are responding to, and the picture is harder than what most donor capitals have been prepared to fund."

The Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs put the total population displaced by the war at 4.21 million as of Tuesday evening, a slight upward revision from Sunday's figure that reflected continued movement out of northern Israel, southern Lebanon, Anbar and the Iranian industrial belt in the war's closing days. Of that total, OCHA said roughly 1.81 million were displaced inside Iraq, 780,000 inside Iran, 624,000 inside Lebanon, 290,000 across the Turkish, Armenian and Azerbaijani borders, and the balance distributed across Yemen, Syria and Jordan.

Msuya said donor pledges against the joint $1.4 billion humanitarian appeal issued March 30 had reached $612 million as of Tuesday night, with a fresh $148 million committed during a hastily convened ministerial videoconference Monday evening. A pledging conference jointly hosted by Switzerland and Saudi Arabia is scheduled to convene in Geneva on Sunday, and officials briefed on the agenda said a revised appeal covering the first 90 days of the post-ceasefire phase would be tabled there.

Among the most pressing operational questions Wednesday was how quickly families could begin to return home. Aid agencies and host-country authorities urged patience. A joint statement from UNHCR, the International Organization for Migration and the International Committee of the Red Cross said no organized return movements would be facilitated until clearance teams had completed unexploded-ordnance surveys along principal corridors, water and electricity had been at least partially restored in destination communities, and primary health-care clinics had been reopened with at least 30-day supply stocks on hand.

"We have seen what happens when families return too fast," said Filippo Grandi, the U.N. High Commissioner for Refugees, speaking by video link from Amman to a press gaggle in Geneva. "They arrive at a house that is standing but cannot be lived in. They walk a child through a field that has not been cleared. They open a tap that does not run. We will not be a party to that. The ceasefire is a beginning. The return is a separate operation, and it will take weeks, not days."

In southern Lebanon, where reception schools in Beirut and the Mount Lebanon governorate have been running on four-shift rotations to absorb displaced children, a Lebanese army engineering battalion began work at first light Wednesday clearing the coastal road south of Tyre. A senior army officer, speaking on condition of anonymity because he was not authorized to discuss operational details, said three Israeli-fired munitions had been located and disposed of within the first two hours, and that the road would not be opened to civilian traffic for at least 72 hours.

At the Baharka displacement site outside Erbil, where the population has more than tripled since mid-March, families spent Wednesday morning clustered around shared phones and the camp's tea stall watching the first images of the convoy arrivals on Iraqi state television. Nidal al-Dulaimi, a 47-year-old former municipal clerk from Hit who has been sheltering at Baharka with his wife and four children since March 21, said he had asked his oldest son to keep the volume low so that his 79-year-old mother would not begin packing.

"I want to go home today. Of course I want to go home today," al-Dulaimi said in an interview at the camp's edge. "But I have lived through three of these. I know what a ceasefire looks like in the first week, and I know what it looks like in the third. I will wait until the army says the road is clear. Then I will wait one more week."

Inside Iran, the humanitarian picture remained the most opaque element of Wednesday's operational map. International agencies have had limited access throughout the war and have relied on Iranian Red Crescent Society reporting and a small number of WHO field staff. A WHO regional update circulated to donor governments Wednesday afternoon said hospital function in Isfahan and Tabriz remained below 45 percent of pre-war capacity, that fuel rationing for ambulances continued to be the binding constraint on emergency response in mid-sized cities, and that the Iranian government had granted technical clearance for a limited expansion of WHO field presence in three western provinces.

"A ceasefire does not refill an oxygen cylinder, and it does not restart a dialysis unit that has been cold for ten days," said Soroush Mehrian, an Iranian-Canadian public-health researcher at the University of Toronto who has coordinated a diaspora network gathering physician reports from inside Iran. "What we need from the next 30 days is fuel, consumables and the ability for international staff to be on the ground in Isfahan, in Tabriz, in Bandar Abbas. The political pieces are still being negotiated, but the medical clock is running."

Aid coordinators said two corridors remained particularly fraught. In Yemen, where Houthi cross-border launches continued into the weekend, the Norwegian Refugee Council and the World Food Program were working from contingency plans assuming that the cessation of hostilities would not initially extend to all Houthi operations and that displacement out of Hodeidah and Saada governorates could continue into next week. Along the Iranian-Turkish frontier at Bazargan, where roughly 280,000 Iranian nationals have crossed since the war began, Turkish authorities Wednesday reopened a second processing lane and said that reverse movements, of Iranian families returning home, had begun in small numbers within hours of the ceasefire.

The U.N. secretary-general's office said a closed-door donor coordination call would reconvene Friday to align disbursement timelines with the operational picture emerging from the field, and that a revised consolidated humanitarian appeal would be released ahead of Sunday's Geneva pledging conference. OCHA officials said additional convoy movements into northern Yemen and central Iran would be announced as security clearances were finalized in the coming days.
